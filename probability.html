Probability

The quiet prerequisite every syllabus assumes and none of them teach. Four weeks to own distributions, expectation, and Bayes at the level a first Bachelor's course demands — then the specific probabilistic machinery the quantum and theory tracks already lean on: the Born rule as a distribution, density matrices as a mixture, and the concentration bounds behind every randomized algorithm.

de Wolf's own prerequisite list, verbatim: linear algebra, probability, discrete math, algorithms
Probability theory is nothing but common sense reduced to calculation.

Why This Track

This one is short on purpose. Logic and Theory of Computation are cathedrals; Probability is scaffolding — real, load-bearing, and not meant to take a year. Every quantum computing course prerequisite list names it in the same breath as linear algebra, and for good reason: the Born rule is a probability distribution, mixed states are probabilistic combinations of pure ones, and the randomized-algorithm chapters of the Theory of Computation track lean on Markov and Chernoff bounds you have not yet derived. Four weeks, two phases: first the general machinery any first Bachelor's course would give you, then the specific slice that the rest of this cathedral already assumes you walked in with.

Track Breakdown
1Weeks 1–2

Foundations — Spaces, Variables, Distributions

▾

Two weeks to build the standard machinery from the axioms up: what a probability space actually is, the handful of distributions that recur everywhere in computer science, and the two ideas — conditioning and independence — that everything else in this cathedral's probabilistic reasoning is built from. None of this is exotic; all of it is assumed.

Probability Spaces & the Kolmogorov Axioms
Blitzstein & Hwang Ch. 1 · Ross Ch. 2

A sample space Ω, an event space (a σ-algebra when Ω is infinite), and a function P assigning each event a number in [0,1] with P(Ω) = 1 and countable additivity over disjoint events. Everything else — union bounds, inclusion-exclusion, the complement rule — falls out of these three axioms by pure logic, not extra assumptions. Get comfortable translating word problems into (Ω, events, P) before reaching for a formula; most probability errors are modeling errors, not arithmetic errors.

You must be able to:
  • State the three Kolmogorov axioms and derive the complement and union-bound rules from them
  • Translate a word problem into an explicit sample space and event
  • Compute with basic combinatorics (permutations, combinations, the multiplication rule) inside a probability calculation
Random Variables & the Standard Discrete Family
Blitzstein & Hwang Ch. 3 · Ross Ch. 4

A random variable is a function from outcomes to numbers — nothing more mystical than that. Master the PMF and CDF machinery on the five distributions that show up constantly in algorithm analysis and quantum computing alike: Bernoulli, Binomial, Geometric, Poisson, and the discrete Uniform. Know not just the formulas but the story each one tells — a Binomial is "how many successes in n independent trials," a Geometric is "how long until the first success" — because recognizing the story is how you actually solve a new problem.

You must be able to:
  • Write the PMF of each of the five named distributions from memory, with its story
  • Derive the Binomial PMF from first principles (counting + independence)
  • Recognize which named distribution a new word problem is secretly asking about
Continuous Random Variables — Uniform, Exponential, Normal
Blitzstein & Hwang Ch. 5 · Ross Ch. 5

The jump from PMF to PDF: probability is no longer read off directly, it is integrated. The Uniform, Exponential, and Normal distributions are the three you need cold — the Exponential for its memorylessness (the one continuous analogue of the Geometric's defining property), the Normal because the Central Limit Theorem is about to explain why it appears everywhere without being asked to.

You must be able to:
  • Move fluently between PDF, CDF, and probability-of-an-interval for a continuous random variable
  • State and use the memoryless property of the Exponential distribution
  • Standardize a Normal random variable and read tail probabilities from it
Expectation, Variance & the Law of Large Numbers
Blitzstein & Hwang Ch. 4, §10.1 · Ross Ch. 4, §8.1

Expectation is linear even when the underlying variables are not independent — E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] always, a fact that does more work in this cathedral than almost any other single line of mathematics, because it lets you compute the expected size of something complicated by decomposing it into simple indicator variables. Variance measures spread; the Law of Large Numbers is the theorem that turns "expected value" from an abstraction into a promise about what long-run averages actually do.

You must be able to:
  • Prove linearity of expectation and use it to compute an expectation via indicator variables
  • Compute Var(X) via E[X²] − E[X]² and know how variance behaves under independent sums
  • State the (weak) Law of Large Numbers and explain, in words, what it does and does not guarantee
Joint Distributions, Independence & Bayes' Theorem
Blitzstein & Hwang Chs. 2, 7 · Jaynes Ch. 2

Conditional probability P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B) is the whole engine; independence is the special case where conditioning changes nothing. Bayes' theorem is not a separate rule to memorize — it is conditional probability applied twice and rearranged — but it is the single most consequential rearrangement in the subject: it is how you go from P(evidence | hypothesis), which you can usually compute, to P(hypothesis | evidence), which is what you actually want to know. Confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A) is the most common error in the field; drill until the distinction is automatic.

You must be able to:
  • Derive Bayes' theorem from the definition of conditional probability in two lines
  • Solve a base-rate problem correctly (the classic "rare disease, imperfect test" trap) and explain why the naive answer is wrong
  • Distinguish independence from conditional independence with a worked counterexample

2Weeks 3–4

The Quantum Interface — Probability Wearing a Lab Coat

▾

This is the phase the whole track exists for. The Born rule, mixed states, and randomized-algorithm analysis are not new mathematics — they are Phase 1's machinery, recognized in disguise. Two weeks to make that recognition automatic, so that when Physics hands you a projective measurement or Theory of Computation hands you a randomized algorithm, the probability underneath is not a second thing to learn.

The Born Rule Is a Probability Distribution
Preskill Ph219 §2.1 · Nielsen & Chuang §2.2.3

Measuring a state |ψ⟩ = Σᵢ αᵢ|i⟩ in the {|i⟩} basis returns outcome i with probability |αᵢ|² — and that assignment is a genuine probability distribution: it is non-negative and, because |ψ⟩ is normalized, it sums to 1. Every tool from Phase 1 applies without modification: you can compute the expected value of a measurement outcome by linearity, and the variance of a measurement is an honest variance. The postulate is not "physics probability, different from math probability" — it is the same object, restricted to amplitudes-squared.

You must be able to:
  • Verify that {|αᵢ|²} is a valid probability distribution for any normalized |ψ⟩
  • Compute E[outcome] and Var(outcome) for a measurement in a given basis, using ordinary expectation machinery
  • Explain why normalization of |ψ⟩ is exactly the same requirement as ΣP(x) = 1
Density Matrices — Mixed States Are Probabilistic Mixtures
Nielsen & Chuang §2.4 · Preskill Ph219 §2.4

A pure state is certainty about which vector you have; a mixed state is genuine classical uncertainty about which pure state you have, exactly the way a random variable is uncertainty about which value you get. If a system is in state |ψᵢ⟩ with classical probability pᵢ, the density matrix ρ = Σᵢ pᵢ|ψᵢ⟩⟨ψᵢ| packages that whole probability distribution into one operator: tr(ρ) = 1 is Σpᵢ = 1 in disguise, and tr(ρ²) ≤ 1 with equality iff the state is pure measures exactly how much genuine (non-quantum) uncertainty is mixed in. This is Phase 1's "distribution over outcomes" promoted one level, to a distribution over states.

You must be able to:
  • Build ρ from a given classical mixture of pure states and verify tr(ρ) = 1
  • Distinguish a mixed state from a superposition — same math notation, different physical meaning — with a worked example
  • Use tr(ρ²) to tell a pure state from a genuinely mixed one
Markov, Chebyshev & Chernoff — Concentration for Algorithms
Mitzenmacher & Upfal Chs. 3–4

Three inequalities, increasingly sharp, all built from the expectation and variance machinery of Phase 1: Markov's inequality bounds P(X ≥ a) using only E[X]; Chebyshev sharpens it using Var(X); Chernoff bounds go further still for sums of independent variables, giving the exponentially small failure probabilities behind every randomized algorithm's correctness guarantee — including the ones in the Theory of Computation track's randomized-complexity phases. These are not new probability; they are Phase 1's tools, aimed at proving something is unlikely rather than computing what it equals.

You must be able to:
  • State and prove Markov's inequality from the definition of expectation
  • Derive Chebyshev's inequality from Markov's, applied to (X − E[X])²
  • State a Chernoff bound for a sum of independent Bernoulli trials and explain what "exponentially small failure probability" buys a randomized algorithm

Where This Fits

This track is scaffolding for three others at once. The Born rule and density matrices in Phase 2 are the exact probabilistic content behind the measurement postulate in Physics and the mixed-state formalism the Quantum Computing track uses for noisy qubits and partial-trace arguments.

Markov, Chebyshev, and Chernoff are the machinery behind every randomized-complexity result in Theory of Computation — BPP, amplification by repetition, and any argument that says "this fails with negligible probability" is secretly a Chernoff bound. And expectation itself, linear before it is anything else, is the same linear-algebraic idea the Linear Algebra track makes precise: a probability distribution is a vector, and expectation is a linear functional.
