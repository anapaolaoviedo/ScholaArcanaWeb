Field VIII · The Memory · A Track for Life

History

From the Greeks to the summer of 1914, with Rome, its fall, the Middle Ages and the Renaissance at the heart. Twelve Books with named sources, exact passages, historians to read and essays to write, read like a historian. No calendar: a Book takes as long as it takes.

Historia vero testis temporum, lux veritatis, vita memoriae, magistra vitae, nuntia vetustatis.
— Cicero, De Oratore 2.36 · history, the witness of the ages, the light of truth, the life of memory, the teacher of life
ad fontes: back to the sources
Why This Track Exists

You found out in one lecture that history is not the story of what happened. It is the discipline of how we know what happened: who wrote this, when, for whom, and why. A chronicle is an argument. A biography is propaganda until proven otherwise. A king's genealogy tells you more about the man who wrote it than about the king.

That is a proof-checker's instinct, applied to the human past. So this track is built like the others: named sources, exact passages, historians who disagree, and essays due. It is modelled on a real first-year history module (Edge Hill's HIS1022, The Medieval World), and it keeps that module's habit of setting a primary source beside a modern historian every single time. It has no end date, because it is not for a semester. It is for life.

The Historian's Questions · Ask Them of Every Source
  1. Who wrote this, and what do we know about them? (Is the author even who we think? Remember "Nennius".)
  2. When was it written, and how far from the events it describes?
  3. For whom: a king, a monastery, a patron, posterity?
  4. Why: to praise, to blame, to legitimise, to console, to warn?
  5. What is missing, and whose voice is not in the room?
  6. What do historians say about it, and why do they say that, when they said it?

The Pilgrimage

Each Book names a place to stand in. Walls, cathedrals, galleries and archives: the past is still lying around Britain.

On the citations: ancient and medieval texts are cited by book · chapter · section (e.g. Bede 2.13, Tacitus, Annals 1.1–10). These numbers are the same in every translation and edition, which is why historians use them instead of page numbers. Modern books are cited by chapter or part, since page numbers change between printings. Any university library search, or a free online translation, will take you straight to the passage.

Where This Fits

History is where your two selves meet. Valla catching a forgery by its Latin is proof by contradiction. Anselm and Aquinas are logic you can formalize. Leibniz's Calculemus! leads to Boole, Frege, Hilbert and then Turing, who answered Ada Lovelace by name. And Butterfield's warning against teleology is the discipline you'll need when you write the history of your own field, or when someone someday writes yours. Read the past for what it was, not for what it became. That is the whole craft.
